\begin{abstract}
FDA summarizes each drug-manufacturing inspection with a three-level grade,
while the investigator's Form 483 observations record what was actually
found. We used a large language model to convert 1,067 observations from 246
inspections at 98 facilities into 11 coded fields and 17 inspection-level
signals. On 50 held-out observations the model agreed with a blinded
annotator on 79.5\% of field labels (95\% CI 74.7--83.8\%), against 86.4\%
(79.5--91.8\%) for the annotator relabeling his own earlier work. Factual
flags were stable across two models (99.4\% agreement on repeat findings),
while inferred causes were not (55.5\% on root cause), a difficulty the
annotator shared. For 160 drug-quality inspections with a known grade, the
grade ordered the text in the expected direction but explained at most
8.7\% (median 4.2\%) of the variation in any signal, and 39\% of Voluntary
Action Indicated inspections had as large a share of critical or major
findings as the median Official Action Indicated inspection. In exploratory
analyses, neither the text nor the grade anticipated a rise in serious
adverse-event reports after an inspection. For reports describing a
defective product, the text scored a median AUC of 0.54 over 100
facility-grouped partitions against 0.43 for the grade, and 0.61 against
0.46 using only findings about the drug itself; this held when the most
debatable report category was removed. Patient behavior in commercial
claims and independent laboratory tests showed no association that survived
correction. The results support the extracted text as a measurement of
inspection content that the grade does not carry; the outcome associations
are leads for a pre-specified test, not established effects.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} FDA Form 483; drug manufacturing; inspection outcomes;
regulatory text; large language models; adverse-event reports

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Generic drugs account for most prescriptions filled in the United States
\citep{fda2023generic}. Manufacturing quality failures can disrupt supply and
expose patients to defective products \citep{fox2014shortage}. The FDA
periodically inspects manufacturing facilities, but researchers generally
observe only fragments of what an inspection finds.

At the close of an inspection, an investigator may issue Form 483, a numbered
set of narrative observations about conditions that may violate current good
manufacturing practice. The agency later assigns No Action Indicated (NAI),
Voluntary Action Indicated (VAI), or Official Action Indicated (OAI), taking
account of the establishment inspection report and the firm's response.
Those documents are not systematically available in our data, so the grade
cannot be reconstructed from the Form 483 alone. The observation text
records what an investigator wrote; the grade compresses the agency's
subsequent assessment into three levels.

We ask three questions. First, can the content of Form 483 observations be
measured reliably at scale? We treat a language-model pipeline as a
measurement instrument and validate it against a blinded annotator, against
the annotator's own consistency, across two models, and against keyword
rules. Second, how much of that content does the three-level grade carry?
This is a question about information, not about whether any grade was
correct. Third, in exploratory analyses, does the text relate to what
happens after the inspection: adverse-event reports, patient behavior in
commercial claims, and independent laboratory tests of the products?

The answers are uneven, and we report them that way. The measurement is
close to the annotator's own consistency on most fields and weakest where
the question requires inference. The grade carries little of the text. The
text does not anticipate general adverse-event reports better than the
grade, but it does better on reports that a product itself was defective,
the outcome closest to what an inspection examines. Because we reached that
outcome after examining others, we present it as a lead for a pre-specified
test. Spontaneous reports do not measure incidence or establish that a
product from a specific site caused an event.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:lit}

\subsection{Regulatory text as data}

Pazhayattil and colleagues counted FDA's public Form 483 observations for
drug manufacturing sites from 2014 to 2018 by the CFR subpart cited
\citep{Pazhayattil2020}. They reported yearly trends and correlations
across five annual totals: laboratory controls and records were the most
cited subparts and moved together, which they attributed to data-integrity
scrutiny without being able to test it from section counts. We extend that
work from yearly counts of cited sections to the content of each
observation, relate that content to the grade of each inspection, and
relate it to outcomes after the inspection. Natural-language methods have
also been applied to drug labels and regulatory narratives
\citep{Bayer2021,Wu2021BERT}. Li and
colleagues validated language-model extraction against human annotation
before using it in three medical-device applications: reproducing a manual
review of device summaries, re-coding adverse event reports whose
manufacturer-assigned codes were often inconsistent with the narrative, and
relating pre-market features to the time until a device's first adverse
event report \citep{Li2026LLMDevice}. Our design follows that
validation-first logic. The second application has a limited analogue here.
FDA's inspection classification rests on the full establishment inspection
report, which weighs the observations against the firm's proposed corrective
measures and the evidence collected \citep{Pazhayattil2020}, and on agency
policy. We observe none of these, so
we do not treat disagreement between the text and the grade as an error in
the grade. We ask instead how much of the content of the observations a
three-level grade can carry. The third application corresponds to our
exploratory outcome analyses, which relate post-approval inspection
findings to later safety reports, patient behavior and laboratory tests. A keyword comparison tests whether contextual coding
actually changes what is measured.

\subsection{Inspections and safety outcomes}

Inspection and citation patterns have been associated with drug shortages
\citep{Stomberg2018,Wang2025MSOM}. FAERS reports are used for
pharmacovigilance, but spontaneous reporting, missing product identifiers,
and reporting differences between products complicate facility-level use
\citep{Rahman2017FAERS,Alatawi2018,Potter2025}. We use FAERS reports as a
noisy downstream correlate, not a causal patient-harm count, and separate
reports whose reaction terms describe a defective product from serious
reports in general. Our relative outcome compares each facility with its
own preceding reporting window. It cannot remove changes in exposure,
publicity, reporting behavior, or the product mix.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Corpus and analysis sample}

The Redica-sourced corpus contains 1,067 observations from 246 inspections
at 98 facilities, from 16 January 2018 to 21 April 2026. The facilities are
those behind the 14 drugs in Valisure's original testing program. All
extraction and validation results use the full corpus. The grade and
outcome analyses use the 213 inspections (985 observations, 93 facilities)
that FDA graded under its Drug Quality Assurance program. The other 33 were
pre-approval visits for a new product (15), clinical-study or
adverse-event-reporting audits (7), device inspections (6), blood, vaccine
or animal-drug inspections (4), or carried no human-drug tag (1). Of the
213, 125 were graded VAI, 24 OAI and 11 NAI; 53 had no grade posted.

\subsection{Extraction validation and reproducibility}
\label{sec:validation}

One annotator blindly labeled 50 observations in round 1. Those labels
exposed a contamination mutual-exclusivity error and ambiguities in patient
risk, data integrity, scope, and severity. The rules and code were then
revised on those same observations. Round 1 is consequently a development
set, and its agreement figures in Table~\ref{tab:human} are not independent
validation. The stratified, blinded round-2 set contains 50 new observations
and 20 embedded repeats, labeled under rules frozen beforehand
(Table~\ref{tab:round2}). On the 50 new observations Claude agreed with
the annotator on 79.5\% of 550 field labels (95\% CI 74.7--83.8\%,
bootstrap over observations). Because the rare-label strata were
oversampled, we also weighted the 50 rows back to the corpus distribution
by post-stratification, which gave 81.2\% (76.1--85.8\%). The 20-row
random stratum alone gave 79.1\%.

The annotator relabeled the 20 repeats four to six weeks after round 1
without access to his earlier labels and matched himself on 86.4\% of 220
field labels (79.5--91.8\%). This is the available human ceiling. It
measures consistency, not correctness, and a single annotator provides no
interrater benchmark. Four rules (data integrity, patient risk, and the two
contamination flags) were amended between rounds, so some test--retest
disagreement on those fields reflects the rule change. For comparison,
Li et al.\ report about 80\% LLM agreement with expert labels and 76--91\%
agreement between two human annotators on a comparable regulatory
extraction task \citep{Li2026LLMDevice}.

Severity was the weakest field: 52.0\% on the four raw tiers (38.5--65.2\%)
against 80\% test--retest. The errors are directional. Claude graded 10 of
the 20 observations the annotator called Major as Critical, and 5 of 27
Moderate observations as Minor; the latter came from the stratum sampled
because Claude had labeled them Minor. On the Critical-or-Major versus
Moderate-or-Minor split that the downstream feature uses
(\texttt{severity\_critmajor\_share}), agreement was 82.0\% (69.2--90.2\%).
Per-field intervals are wide at $n=50$, and the rarest classes (one Minor
observation by the annotator's labels) cannot be evaluated.

The annotator also returned a second copy after checking his labels with
Claude. We do not use it: of its 24 categorical edits, 18 moved his label
to the Claude extraction's value, so it is not independent of the model
under evaluation.

As a descriptive benchmark, multiplying each model's self-agreement gives
67.9\% for root cause against the observed 55.5\% cross-model agreement,
and 85.5\% for remediation against 48.2\%. These products come from the
\texttt{reproducibility\_vs\_crossmodel.csv} rates and are not a formal
null model: error prevalence, correlated mistakes, and differences between
the 50-row and full-corpus samples matter. The gaps nevertheless show why
a category assigned by one model should not automatically be treated as an
invariant property of the observation. Binary presence flags, particularly
repeat and confirmed contamination, were more portable.

We read this as a property of the fields, not of one model. Root cause is
the field that requires the most inference from the text, and it was the
least stable for every reader: GPT-5-mini agreed with itself on 73\% of
repeated root-cause labels, and our annotator agreed with himself on 75\%
of 20 hidden repeats (95\% CI 53--89\%). Kappa, which adjusts for the
number and balance of categories, gives the same ordering (0.95 for repeat
finding against 0.37 for root cause). The comparison involves two models,
so we do not claim it generalizes to every model.

The v2 schema was revised on the 50 round-1 development observations and
then applied unchanged to both models. A single schema keeps the model
comparison from becoming a comparison of prompts, but it means agreement
on the development observations reflects fit to the data used to build the
instrument, and we report it only as such. Claude Sonnet 5 was designated
the production model before the held-out round. On the 50 held-out
observations, which informed neither the schema nor that choice, Claude
agreed with the annotator on 79.5\% of field-level labels (550 labels;
95\% CI 74.7--83.8\%) and GPT-5-mini on 76.2\% (71.1--80.9\%). The
intervals overlap; the largest difference was root cause, 68\% against
56\%. All outcome analyses use Claude Sonnet 5 with the v2 schema. One of
the 17 outcome features, the share of culturally attributed root causes,
rests on the weakest field.

\subsection{What the observations describe}

Claude assigned Facilities and Equipment to 301 of 1,067 observations
(28.2\%), Laboratory Controls to 279 (26.1\%), and Quality System to
261 (24.5\%). Moderate severity was most common (462 of 1,067; 43.3\%),
followed by Major (397; 37.2\%) and Critical (190; 17.8\%).
Data-integrity findings occurred in 208 observations (19.5\%), confirmed
contamination in 93 (8.7\%), and contamination-control risk without a
confirmed event in 462 (43.3\%). Table~\ref{tab:descriptive} gives the
complete distributions and Wilson intervals. These are model-coded
descriptions conditional on Claude Sonnet 5 and the v2 schema.

Data-integrity findings were concentrated in laboratory controls. Of the
208 observations Claude flagged for data integrity, 109 (52.4\%) were
laboratory-control observations, and 39.1\% of all laboratory-control
observations involved data integrity, against 24.1\% in the quality system,
15.0\% in production and 4.0\% in facilities and equipment. At the level of
individual observations, this supports the explanation Pazhayattil and
colleagues proposed for the co-citation of laboratory controls and records
in yearly counts \citep{Pazhayattil2020}.

\subsection{Contextual extraction versus keyword rules}

On the 1,067-observation Redica corpus, the keyword rule flagged data
integrity in 5.4\%, while Claude flagged it in 19.5\%; 15.0 percentage
points were Claude-only (Table~\ref{tab:regex}). For contamination,
the lexical rule matched 30.4\% of observations but could not distinguish
a confirmed event from a control gap. Claude classified 8.7\% as confirmed
and 43.3\% as risk without confirmation. The regex-only share was 23.8
percentage points when compared with confirmed contamination, showing the
cost of equating contamination vocabulary with an observed event.

For investigation and repeat findings, the two methods agreed on 90.3\%
(n=1,067; 95\% CI 88.4--92.0\%) and 98.3\% (n=1,067; 97.3--98.9\%),
respectively. A simple rule is competitive for these lexically marked
concepts. Table~\ref{tab:regex} measures agreement between methods, not
comparative accuracy against a held-out human standard.

\subsection{How much of the text the inspection grade carries}
\label{sec:grade}

For the 160 drug-quality inspections with a known grade (125 VAI, 24 OAI,
11 NAI; 87 facilities), the grade orders the text in the expected direction.
OAI inspections had the highest mean share of Critical or Major observations
(0.69, against 0.52 for VAI and 0.30 for NAI) and of confirmed
contamination (0.17, 0.06 and 0.02). Yet the grade explained little of the
variation. Across the 17 inspection-level signals, the between-grade share
of variance was at most 8.7\% (multi-domain findings) and had a median of
4.2\%. Inspections with the same grade differed widely: 39\% of VAI
inspections had a Critical-or-Major share at or above the OAI median, and
48\% had findings spanning three or more systems, as 88\% of OAI
inspections did.
This is a descriptive statement about information content. It does not
imply that any grade was assigned incorrectly.

\subsection{Exploratory outcome analyses}
\label{sec:outcomes}

\paragraph{Linking inspections to adverse-event reports.} FAERS names the
approved application (ANDA) of a reported product, not the facility that
made it. We kept only ANDAs that a single facility makes anywhere in the
ProPublica product-to-facility file (37,584 NDCs, 7,507 ANDAs, 723
facilities). Of 6,522 single-facility ANDAs, 2,958 are made at 90 corpus
facilities and 2,262, at 88 facilities, have at least one serious FAERS
case (median 29 ANDAs per facility). We defined two kinds of reports. All
serious reports are dominated by side effects and other patient outcomes.
Product-defect reports are those whose MedDRA reaction terms describe the
product itself, such as contamination, odour, colour, physical defects,
potency, packaging, counterfeiting or recall; 88,019 reports carried at
least one such term. For each inspection, the outcome is whether reports at
the facility rose in the four quarters after the inspection compared with
the four before. Of the 213 drug-quality inspections, 163 qualified for the
serious-report outcome (129 with a known grade) and 131 for the
product-defect outcome (102 with a known grade). Each AUC below is the
median over 100 random facility-grouped five-fold partitions, with all
feature sets scored on the same partitions.

\paragraph{General serious reports.} Neither the text nor the grade
anticipated a rise in serious reports (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Where the
grade was known, the OAI flag returned 0.539 (2.5th--97.5th percentile
0.523--0.555) and text 0.497 under logistic regression (0.427--0.576) and
0.453 under random forest (0.385--0.529); text exceeded the flag on 19\%
and 3\% of partitions. On all 163 inspections text returned 0.523 and
0.510 against 0.525. Counting each ANDA separately, so that one large
product cannot decide a facility's outcome, gave no text advantage where
the grade was known (0.489 and 0.506 against 0.539); on all 163
inspections the random forest reached 0.574 against 0.524. We do not read
this mixed pattern as support.

Laboratory-control findings tracked the level of reports. In 64 unadjusted
feature-by-quarter Spearman tests, 34 had $p<0.05$ and 4 passed the
Bonferroni threshold; the strongest was the count of laboratory-control
observations against reports nine to twelve months later ($\rho=0.321$,
$n=154$). Holding fixed the number of the facility's single-site ANDAs left
3 significant after correction ($\rho=0.332$). Holding fixed the facility's
own reports in the four quarters before the inspection left none, and only
3 below 0.05 ($\rho=0.178$, $p=0.027$). Laboratory findings therefore mark
facilities with many reports, not facilities whose reports are about to
rise. Reports rose after 53.0\% of VAI inspections (53 of 100), 68.4\% of
OAI inspections (13 of 19) and 50.0\% of NAI inspections (5 of 10); VAI and
OAI did not differ (Fisher exact $p=0.31$), and the direction of the gap has
changed with earlier counting choices. Within VAI, the text did not separate
rises from non-rises (random-forest AUC 0.385, permutation $p=0.93$).

\paragraph{Product-defect reports.} For reports describing a defective
product, the text did better than the grade (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
Where the grade was known, text returned 0.535 under logistic regression
against 0.433 for the OAI flag and 0.422 for the three-class grade, higher
than the flag on 97\% of partitions; random forest returned 0.488, higher
on 84\%. Restricting the text to findings in the product-facing systems
(Production, Laboratory Controls, Materials, Packaging and Labeling)
before aggregation sharpened the result: on 112 inspections, random forest
returned 0.613 (0.559--0.666) against 0.458, higher on every partition. On
the same 112 inspections, the restriction raised random forest from 0.502
to 0.613 and logistic regression from 0.496 to 0.574, higher on 99\% and
98\% of partitions. For serious reports in general the same restriction
helped on only 36\% of partitions.

The grade scored below 0.5 for this outcome: product-defect reports tended
not to rise after an OAI grade. The result held when we removed the most
debatable report category, problems attributed to a switch between
manufacturers, which may reflect patient perception. Without it, text
returned 0.569 against 0.429 where the grade was known (higher on every
partition) and 0.554 against 0.469 for product-system findings (93\%).
Removing reports of patches that do not adhere changed nothing, because no
such reports fall on these facilities' products. These AUCs are modest, and
we reached this outcome after the general serious-report analyses above;
it should be pre-specified and tested on new inspections before it is
treated as a finding.

Following the design of \citet{Li2026LLMDevice}, we also modeled the time
from each inspection to a product's first defect report, for 2,748
ANDA-inspection pairs at 78 facilities (875 events). No text signal
survived Holm correction across 17 signals, and the OAI flag was not
associated with timing either (hazard ratio 1.14, 95\% CI 0.88--1.50).

\paragraph{Patient behavior and laboratory tests.} Two further outcomes did
not support the text. In commercial and Medicare claims (MarketScan), we
ranked each company's exact product by how often patients stopped refilling
after being switched to it, against other companies making the same
product. Across 136 products bridged to 31 corpus facilities, a one-SD
higher data-integrity share went with a 11.7 percentile-point worse rank
(95\% CI 5.2--18.2; Holm $p=0.002$) and a laboratory-control share with
9.9 points (3.4--16.5). The data-integrity estimate fell to 9.0 points
(0.3--17.6) without the adjustment for patients who lost coverage, did not
carry into 2023--2024 (2.0 points, $-7.9$ to 11.9), and was 1.4 points
($-1.9$ to 4.7) against the MarketScan team's own product quality score,
for which none of the 17 signals survived Holm correction. Against
Valisure's laboratory scores for 619 tested samples linked to 64 corpus
facilities, none of 85 signal-outcome tests survived correction, and adding
the text did not improve prediction of the laboratory score.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The clearest results concern measurement. A language model can code Form
483 observations close to the consistency of a trained annotator, and the
fields that resist it are the ones that resist the annotator too. Factual
flags such as repeat findings and confirmed contamination transfer across
models; inferred causes do not, so a root-cause distribution from one
model should not be read as a property of manufacturing practice without
this check. Held-out agreement, not agreement on the observations used to
write the rules, is the relevant accuracy figure, and it places the
production model a few points from the human ceiling.

The grade carries little of what the investigator wrote. It orders the
text sensibly, with more severe and more contamination-related findings at
OAI inspections, but three levels cannot represent the spread within each
level. Many VAI inspections read like OAI ones. This does not imply an
error in FDA's grading: the grade serves a regulatory decision and draws on
the establishment inspection report, the firm's response and agency
policy, none of which we observe. It does mean that research using the
grade as a measure of what an inspection found discards most of the
recorded content.

The outcome analyses are exploratory. For serious adverse-event reports in
general, neither the text nor the grade anticipated a rise; the
laboratory-control association with report levels disappears once each
facility is compared with its own history, which points to facility size
and product mix rather than an early warning. For reports describing a
defective product, the outcome closest to what an inspection examines, the
text did better than the grade, and most clearly with findings about the
product-facing systems. That pattern is what one would expect if the text
measures product quality, but we chose this outcome after the general
analyses, the AUCs are modest, and the result needs a pre-specified test on
new inspections. Patient behavior after a switch and independent laboratory
tests did not support the text once counting choices and multiple testing
were accounted for.

The main limitation is outcome attribution. FAERS records suspected
products and application numbers, not the manufacturing site for a given
patient's unit. The single-site restriction removes known multi-site ANDAs,
but a matched report is still not verified manufacturing-caused harm.
FAERS is passive surveillance, lacks a population denominator, and may
change with reporting practices. The restriction also selects facilities
with uniquely linked products and adequate report history, and the
product-to-facility file is itself incomplete. The text corpus covers only
the facilities behind the original 14 ingredients; Valisure has since
tested 43, and adding Form 483 text for the facilities on that larger list
is the most direct way to enlarge the sample. A second annotator, held-out
calibration, and a pre-registered product-defect analysis are needed before
stronger claims.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Sources and observation extraction}

The facility universe was assembled from the Valisure API-to-FEI map for
14 ingredients. Redica Systems supplied licensed, digitized Form 483
narratives for 98 of the facilities. Each inspection is a facility and the
483's issue date. Its grade comes from Redica's site event file: the
``Drug Quality Assurance'' classification on an inspection event at the
same site and exact date. Inspections graded only under another FDA
program (devices, bioresearch monitoring, pre-approval evaluation, blood
products, animal drugs) or whose 483 carried no human-drug tag were
excluded from the grade and outcome analyses, following the rule used in
earlier work on this facility set. Inspections with no grade posted were
kept as unknown grade. Extraction and validation used all 1,067
observations; the grade and outcome analyses used the 985 observations of
the 213 retained inspections.

\subsection{Observation extraction and validation}

The production extraction used Claude Sonnet 5 and the v2 prompt in
the observation-extraction script; GPT-5-mini received the
same observation text and field rules for the model comparison. Pharmacist
Yelena Ionova's review of the earlier prompt led to FDA six-system
violation categories, a distinction between confirmed contamination and
contamination-control risk, and a high-risk noninjectable patient-risk
scenario. The field rules were further amended after round-1 blinded
annotation. For example, retesting an unfavorable result into compliance
without a scientifically justified investigation is a data-integrity
finding. A confirmed Grade A/B environmental-monitoring excursion can
satisfy the sterile-product patient-risk scenario. A named market
complaint establishes distribution of the named batch, but a general
CFR preamble does not. These decisions are encoded in the prompt and
the annotator protocol, not inferred after viewing the outcome.

Each observation receives one category from Quality System, Production,
Materials, Facilities and Equipment, Laboratory Controls, Packaging and
Labeling, or Other; a severity tier; scope; root-cause type; remediation
signal; and six binary flags. Severity reflects documented product impact:
Critical for affected released product, Major for an observed defect or
unreliable result without documented release, Moderate for a deficient
control without observed defect, and Minor for an administrative gap.
The production model used the share coded Critical or Major. An earlier
feature, Major plus Moderate share, was inversely related to Critical
share ($r=-0.842$) and reached 1.0 on 54.5\% of snapshots because
Critical was excluded from its numerator. The corrected Critical plus
Major measure is monotonic in the intended severity direction. The
old feature's selection reflected better human agreement at the
Major/Moderate boundary but did not solve this construct problem.

Patient-risk flags require one of four text-supported pathways: confirmed
contamination in a sterile or injectable product, a confirmed defect in a
text-named narrow-therapeutic-index, oncology, or nitrosamine product,
a confirmed defect in a product affirmatively reported as released, or
release without documented quality disposition. Confirmed contamination
and contamination-control risk are mutually exclusive by post-processing.
Investigation failure requires an inadequate investigation of a concrete
event. Repeat finding requires an explicit reference to an earlier
inspection. A separate cross-inspection repeat feature matches the
standardized citation sentence to a prior inspection at the same FEI
using token overlap of at least 0.80. The evidence quote must occur
verbatim in the observation; otherwise the pipeline clears it.

The 50-observation round-1 sample was blinded to model predictions and
spread across facilities. It drove rule changes and is treated as
development data. Round 2 was prepared with 50 new observations,
stratified to include rare labels, plus 20 embedded repeats, shuffled and
renumbered so the annotator could not identify them. A blank remediation
label is scored as the value None on both sides, as the annotator
confirmed it means no remediation was observed. Overall agreement on the
new observations is weighted back to the corpus by post-stratification:
each unlabeled observation is assigned to the first sampling stratum it
matches, and each labeled row is weighted by its stratum's corpus size
over its labeled count. Agreement intervals
for proportions use Wilson's method. Cross-model agreement uses exact
match and Cohen's kappa. The regex baseline applies fixed lexical
patterns to the Redica text, then compares its flags with Claude's
flags on the same 1,067 observations. An earlier Redica export had all
regex fields defaulted to false because the input lacked those columns;
the baseline was recomputed directly from text before this comparison.

\subsection{Facility panel and outcomes}

\texttt{02\_aggregate\_fei\_features.py} computes each inspection's 17 text
features from that inspection's observations. The inspection-centered panel
attaches FAERS counts for the four quarters before and after the visit.
FAERS reports were limited to primary-suspect drugs with an ANDA number.

Attribution uses the ProPublica product-to-facility file. An ANDA is kept
only if a single facility makes it anywhere in that file, judged before
restricting to the corpus, so a second plant outside the corpus cannot be
hidden. Each FAERS case is counted once, in the first quarter it appears;
later versions of the same case (65,781 of 554,509 report-ANDA pairs at
corpus facilities, 11.9\%) are dropped. The serious-report outcome keeps
death, hospitalization, life-threatening event, disability, congenital
anomaly, required intervention and other serious outcomes. The
product-defect outcome keeps reports of any seriousness whose MedDRA
preferred terms (FAERS REAC files, 2015Q1--2026Q1) contain the word
``product'' and are not use or medication-error terms, plus three
manufacturing terms without that word and two manufacturer-side terms the
exclusion would otherwise remove. Of 186 candidate terms, 85 were kept;
the full list with report counts is in the supplement.

For each inspection, the binary target is whether the facility's report
count summed over quarters $+1$ to $+4$ exceeds the sum over quarters
$-4$ to $-1$. A quarter with no record is a true zero. An inspection is
kept only if its facility holds a single-site ANDA, the full window lies
within the FAERS extract (2015Q1--2026Q1), and at least one report falls in
the baseline (quarters $-4$ to $0$). For serious reports, 198 of the 213
inspections are at linked facilities, 178 have the full window, and 163
have a baseline report. In the product-by-product variant, each ANDA with a
baseline report counts as rising or not, and the inspection is scored as a
rise if more than half of them rose.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

The grade comparison uses the 160 inspections with a known grade. For each
of the 17 signals, the share of variance explained by grade is the
between-grade sum of squares over the total; differences are tested with
Kruskal-Wallis.

We compared logistic regression and a random forest in facility-grouped
five-fold cross-validation, so that all inspections from a facility fall in
one fold. Because a single split moved AUCs by up to 0.05 between library
versions, we report the median over 100 random facility-grouped partitions
with the 2.5th--97.5th percentile, scoring every feature set on the same
partitions. These percentiles describe partition sensitivity, not sampling
uncertainty, and the share of partitions on which text beats a baseline is
a robustness summary, not a significance test. FDA baselines are the binary
OAI flag and three-class dummies. The product-system analysis drops
observations outside Production, Laboratory Controls, Materials, and
Packaging and Labeling before aggregation; the paired comparison scores
both versions on the inspections they share.

The lag analysis correlates 16 text features with report counts at the
inspection quarter and three later quarters, 64 Spearman tests with a
Bonferroni threshold of $0.05/64$. Partial Spearman correlations hold fixed
either the number of the facility's single-site ANDAs or its reports in
the four quarters before the inspection. The time-to-event analysis fits a
Cox model per signal for each ANDA at an inspected facility, from the
inspection to its first product-defect report, censored at the next
inspection, eight quarters or 2026Q1, with controls for prior reports and
standard errors clustered by facility; Holm correction runs over the 17
signals. The within-VAI probe uses a 200-shuffle permutation test of the
random-forest AUC.

The MarketScan product analysis regresses a product's within-product
percentile of post-switch abandonment on facility-level text shares with
product fixed effects and facility-clustered standard errors. The Valisure
analysis relates the same 17 signals to the DoD laboratory score and its
components within product. Both apply Holm correction across signals.
